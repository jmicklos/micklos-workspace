Dear Mr. Biggs,

I'm a Capitol Hill resident, a regular Amtrak Cascades passenger on the Portland–Seattle route, and — like effectively every other working passenger on that train — someone whose laptop is useless for most of the journey south of Centralia. I want to be direct: the state of onboard connectivity on Cascades is indefensible on a corridor WSDOT owns the rolling stock for and fully subsidizes operations on.

The technical failure is structural, not a coverage-map story. The current Nomad Digital cellular-aggregation architecture was installed on Cascades in January 2014, and its fundamental design is geometrically mismatched to the corridor it serves. Tower density is optimized for the I-5 freeway and population centers, but the BNSF right-of-way diverges from I-5 through significant stretches of Lewis and Cowlitz counties. The aggregator shares a small number of cellular modems across an entire trainset of passengers, which is a capacity ceiling no amount of additional cellular hardware resolves. And handoffs at 79 mph compound the problem at every sector boundary. Amtrak's own wifi page concedes the underlying limitation: bandwidth available from cell towers along the route is limited. Additional investment in this architecture will not change the outcome.

This is also not a problem passengers can route around individually, and I want to name the lived experience here because I suspect it is undercounted in whatever data WSDOT and Amtrak are looking at. Those of us who know how to configure our own mobile hotspots are caught in exactly the same trap: our phones sit in the same Faraday cage, pointed at the same rural towers that the onboard aggregator can't reach, moving through the same handoff boundaries at the same 79 mph. Metal rolling stock attenuates cellular signal significantly — the UK Department for Transport's measurement study on rail carriages recorded vehicle penetration loss of up to 28 dB, meaning signal reaching a passenger can be hundreds of times weaker than signal outside the car. The result is a quiet exasperation that any working passenger on this train will recognize — oscillating between Amtrak's twelve-year-old aggregator and personal tethering to marginal rural cellular, and getting meaningful work done on neither. There is no self-service workaround. The architecture does not permit one.

The solution is commercially mature and in service today. Brightline in Florida has operated with Starlink for rail since May 2023. Rail-certified satellite integrations are commercially available from multiple vendors — Icomera, a SpaceX Tier 1 authorized Starlink reseller for trains, and Clarus Networks, which offers EN50155/EN50121/EN45545-certified Starlink-for-rail installations. The FCC approved Starlink connectivity on moving vehicles on June 30, 2022. This is not a technology debate; it is a procurement decision.

I want to name the broader issue plainly, because it is the part of this situation that frustrates me most. The Seattle–Portland corridor is one of the densest concentrations of technology workers in North America — Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Boeing, Google, and Expedia on the Seattle side; Intel, Nike, Columbia, and a very large remote workforce on the Portland side. The entire stated value proposition of a subsidized intercity rail service is to capture mode-shift from cars and short-haul flights. The single passenger segment most likely to mode-shift is knowledge workers who would trade a four-hour flight-plus-airport or a 3½-hour drive for three hours of uninterrupted productive time on a train. That is precisely the segment the current service is structurally unable to serve. I would ride Cascades substantially more often than I currently do if the service supported real work, and I can speak for a very large number of peers across both cities who have made the same calculation and chosen their cars or Alaska Airlines.

What should be an economic powerhouse option for the Cascadia corridor is instead a twelve-year-old system that actively starves the region of the economic opportunity this investment was meant to create. WSDOT and ODOT have committed to pursuing precisely that mode-shift, explicitly, in the State Rail Plan and in the Cascades Service Development Plan. Continuing to accept the current product is a policy failure against stated goals, not an unavoidable constraint.

Cascades south of Centralia is close to an ideal test environment for satellite connectivity — open sky, diesel traction, no overhead catenary, minimal tunneling. A single-trainset, 90-day pilot would cost a rounding error against the capital program WSDOT is already running on the corridor, and the incoming Siemens Airo trainsets are the obvious integration window. Once those trainsets are delivered and in service without satellite hardware, the retrofit cost goes up substantially — and the decision effectively locks the corridor into the current architecture for another equipment generation.

I would like a written response on three questions:

1. Is WSDOT actively considering a satellite connectivity pilot on the Cascades corridor, and if so, on what timeline?
2. Has WSDOT evaluated integrating rail-certified satellite terminals into the Airo trainset delivery? If not, why not?
3. Who at WSDOT, or at Amtrak under the operating contract, is the accountable owner for onboard connectivity as a passenger experience metric, and against what performance targets?

I've copied the chairs and ranking members of the House and Senate Transportation Committees, my own 43rd District legislative delegation, and Amtrak's EVP of Digital Technology and Innovation.

Thank you for your time and for the response I trust will follow.
