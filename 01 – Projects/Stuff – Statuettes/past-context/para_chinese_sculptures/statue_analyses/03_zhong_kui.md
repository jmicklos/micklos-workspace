# Statue 3 — Zhong Kui on Demon

## Working Identity
**Zhong Kui (钟馗) — Vanquisher of Demons**  
One of the most recognizable and beloved figures in Chinese religious and folk art. The iconographic combination here is nearly unmistakable.

## Identifying Features (Definitive)
- **Dark/black face:** Zhong Kui is consistently depicted with a dark, fierce complexion
- **Raised fist:** Classic gesture of capturing or striking demons
- **Seated atop a subdued demon/gui (鬼):** The creature beneath — a captured malevolent spirit — is the single most definitive iconographic marker of Zhong Kui
- **Square angular official's cap (幞头):** His characteristic mortarboard-like headgear
- **Civil/scholar robes** (not armor): Flowing robes tied at chest — consistent with his identity as a failed imperial scholar who became the supreme demon-queller
- **Two-figure composition on integrated base:** Classical Zhong Kui format

## Who Is Zhong Kui?
Zhong Kui occupies a unique space in Chinese religion — neither purely Buddhist nor Daoist, but deeply embedded in popular folk religion. According to tradition, he was a brilliant scholar who died by suicide after being unfairly denied first place in the imperial examinations, and was subsequently deified by Tang Emperor Xuanzong as the supreme exorcist and protector against evil spirits. Commonly displayed in homes and temples, especially during the Dragon Boat Festival, to ward off malevolent forces.

## Physical Description
- **Main figure:** Seated/perched male figure in civil robes, left arm raised with fist clenched (attribute lost — likely originally a sword, judge's brush, or bat)
- **Demon (gui):** Crouching creature with wide eyes, flattened nose, open mouth — subdued under Zhong Kui's weight. Remarkably well-preserved relative to main figure.
- **Base:** Integrated decorative cloud/rock base, carved from same block or closely joined — typical of Zhong Kui compositions
- **Scale:** Taller and more compositionally ambitious than Statues 1 and 2

## Key Observations
- **No spirit cavity visible on reverse** — differs from Statues 1, 2, 4, 5. May indicate different workshop tradition or different functional context (domestic vs. temple).
- **Blue pigment remnants:** Expensive pigment, suggests higher-quality commission
- **Integrated base construction:** Demon and cloud base appear carved as unified composition
- **Raised fist has lost its original attribute**

## Condition
- Most extensive paint loss of all five pieces — dark ground layer largely exposed
- Significant wood cracking on base
- Demon figure beneath is notably well-preserved
- No major losses to overall compositional integrity

## Probable Origin & Date
- **Ming dynasty, 16th–17th century**
- Possibly **Jiangnan (Yangtze Delta) or central Chinese workshop** — though Fujian workshops also produced Zhong Kui figures prolifically
- The angular, blocky headdress is characteristic of Ming-period Zhong Kui iconography

## Relationship to Collection
Likely from a **separate altar or temple context** from Statues 1, 2, 4, 5 — different construction (no spirit cavity), different deity type (folk exorcist vs. bureaucratic/martial), different compositional approach. May have been acquired separately. However, shared approximate date and regional tradition.

**Collector note:** Zhong Kui figures with an intact demon base are eagerly collected. The two-figure composition makes this substantially more complex and desirable than a single standing figure.
