# Statue 4 — Seated Civil Deity (Primary Altar Figure)

## Working Identity
**Seated male deity in civil/scholarly robes — probable primary altar deity**  
Most likely: **Wenchang Dijun (文昌帝君)** — God of Culture, Literature, and Scholarly Success.  
Alternatives: Taisui (太岁), Chenghuang (城隍 — City God), or Xuantian Shangdi in civil manifestation.

## Why Wenchang Dijun?
- Consistently depicted in civil robes with a tall crown, seated, calm expression, beard
- One of the most widely venerated deities in Ming-dynasty China — especially in regions with strong examination culture
- The tall structured double-tier crown with comb-like ridges closely matches Wenchang iconography
- Seated central throne posture = primary deity, not attendant

## Physical Description
- **Posture:** Seated on throne, frontal orientation — classic primary deity placement
- **Face:** Bearded, calm, benevolent, slightly smiling — marks of senior male deity status and wisdom
- **Headdress:** Tall structured double-tier crown (guanzhuang 冠状) with fine comb-like decorative ridges — associated with high-ranking Daoist or imperial deities. Distinct from military caps of Statues 1 & 2.
- **Robes:** Long flowing civil robes with belt — no armor
- **Right hand:** Holds an object (partially surviving) — likely originally a ruyi scepter (如意), tablet, or scroll
- **Lower body:** Merges into large solid rectangular throne block — architecturally simplified construction
- **Scale:** Substantially larger than Statues 1, 2, 3 — approximately 50–65cm tall estimated
- **Arms:** Carved separately and pegged in (tenon joints visible) — standard for figures of this scale

## Key Authenticity Markers
- **Spirit cavity (藏腹):** Present on upper back — well-defined rectangular mortise, now open and empty
- **Deep black-brown lacquer ground:** Near-complete loss of surface pigment may indicate greater age or more exposed temple position
- **Crown carving detail:** Despite overall paint loss, comb-like ridges on crown are crisp and well-preserved — indicates skilled workshop carving
- **Monumental lower body:** Large solid rectangular throne block = construction technique associated with earlier Ming or late Yuan figures

## Condition
- Most paint loss of the group — deep black-brown ground largely exposed
- Red and traces of green/teal surviving underneath in areas
- Crown details well-preserved despite surface losses
- Structural integrity appears sound
- Spirit cavity open, contents long gone

## Probable Origin & Date
- **Potentially the earliest piece in the collection** — possibly 15th–16th century Ming, potentially late 14th century
- Indicators of earlier date: simplified monumental lower body throne block; crown form closer to Song-Yuan conventions; overall gravitas and proportional weight; less ornate surface decoration
- Likely **central or northern Fujian**, or possibly **Jiangxi or Hunan** provincial workshop — crown type has parallels in interior Chinese deity sculpture rather than coastal traditions

## Relationship to Collection
**Almost certainly the primary altar deity** that Statues 1, 2, and 5 served:
- Statues 1 & 2 (kneeling warriors) flanked as martial guardians
- Statue 5 (standing attendant with document) flanked as civil attendant/scribe
- This is a classic temple altar configuration

**Note for appraiser:** The scale difference, throne posture, civil robes, and central placement all support primary deity identification. If the ensemble hypothesis is correct, this is the most significant individual piece in the collection.
