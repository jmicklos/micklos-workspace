# Statue 5 — Standing Civil Attendant

## Working Identity
**Standing civil attendant figure — divine scribe, recording official, or ritual secretary**  
Most likely: **Pan Guan (判官)** — divine judge/registrar — or **Wen Pan (文判)**, a civil recording official.  
Alternative: **Lu Xing (禄星)** — Star God of Prosperity and Official Rank, commonly depicted standing in civil robes holding a tablet or scroll.

## Why a Recording Official?
- Both hands raised, holding a rectangular object presented in formal offering/presenting gesture = classic posture of divine official presenting records to a superior deity
- Standing posture = attendant role (primary deities are seated)
- Civil robes + document = administrative/scholarly function, not military
- Positioned in relation to Statue 4 (seated primary deity) as secretary/scribe

## Physical Description
- **Posture:** Standing — the only fully standing figure in the collection. Attendants stand; primary deities are seated. Iconographically significant.
- **Face:** Subtle beard visible; warm, slightly knowing expression with notable psychological character and liveliness. Portrait-like quality — the finest facial carving in the collection.
- **Headdress:** Short, flat futou (幞头) cap variant — associated with civil officials of middle rank. Significant insect damage to crown top.
- **Robes:** Long flowing gown with crossed collar, belt, layered sleeves — purely administrative/scholarly, no armor
- **Hands:** Both raised, holding a rectangular document, register, or tablet in formal presenting gesture
- **Base:** Multi-sided octagonal/faceted stepped base integrated into figure — Ming dynasty standing attendant convention
- **Scale:** Smaller than Statue 4, comparable to Statues 1 & 2

## Key Authenticity Markers
- **Spirit cavity (藏腹):** Present on reverse (image 6) — large, cleanly cut rectangular mortise, well-preserved. Same temple consecration tradition as Statues 1, 2, 4.
- **Insect/worm damage on cap:** Significant worm-track damage visible on headgear top. Authenticity indicator — represents centuries of exposure, extremely difficult to fake convincingly.
- **Surviving polychrome:** Retains more original lacquer than most other pieces — warm reddish-brown ground with gold highlights still visible on face, neck, robe edges.
- **Facial carving quality:** The most individualized, portrait-like face in the collection. Suggests this was carved by the senior craftsman of the workshop.

## Condition
- Best overall paint preservation of the group
- Significant insect damage to crown/cap
- Spirit cavity open, contents long gone
- Structural integrity appears sound
- Some losses at robe hem/base junction

## Probable Origin & Date
- **Ming dynasty, 16th–17th century**
- Standing posture, document-holding gesture, and civil robe type particularly associated with **Fujian and Guangdong** provincial temple sculpture
- Facial carving quality suggests a **higher-tier workshop** than a purely rural village commission

## Relationship to Collection
**Attendant to Statue 4** (seated primary deity). Together with Statues 1 & 2, formed a complete altar flanking ensemble:
- Left flank: one warrior (Statue 1 or 2)
- Right flank: other warrior
- Standing attendant: likely positioned to one side presenting documents/offerings to the primary deity

**Note:** The exceptional facial carving suggests this figure may have been given special attention — possibly because the document-presenting gesture and direct visual relationship to the primary deity made it the most "interactive" element of the ensemble composition.

## Carved Panel (Visible in Background)
A large carved and painted wooden panel is visible in several photos — showing flame or cloud motifs in red, black, and ochre. This appears to be a separate architectural element — possibly a backing panel, altar surround, or temple furniture fragment. Not one of the five statues but potentially from the same temple context. Worth noting for appraiser.
