# Spirit Cavity (藏腹) — Reference Notes

## What Is It?
A spirit cavity (藏腹, zàng fù, literally "hidden belly") is a deliberate hollow mortise cut into the back — usually the upper back or torso — of a consecrated Chinese religious sculpture. It was a standard practice in Chinese Buddhist and Daoist temple sculpture, particularly prevalent in southern Chinese workshop traditions.

## Purpose
The cavity was used to insert sacred consecrating objects at the time of ritual activation (开光, kāi guāng — "opening the light") of the sculpture. Common contents included:
- Handwritten or printed sutras (Buddhist scriptures)
- Daoist talismans or texts
- Grain (rice, millet) — symbolizing sustenance and prosperity
- Coins or precious objects
- Relics or sacred soil
- Small secondary figures

Once filled, the cavity was typically sealed with a wooden panel, lacquered over, and painted to match the surrounding surface.

## Significance for Authentication
The presence of a spirit cavity is a **strong authenticity marker** for several reasons:
1. It indicates the figure was produced for genuine religious use, not as a decorative export or later reproduction
2. The cavity construction (mortise cutting, interior surfaces, aging of exposed wood) must be consistent with overall age of piece
3. The seal/panel is typically lost over time, leaving the cavity open — as in all four examples in this collection
4. Faking a convincing spirit cavity in combination with appropriately aged surrounding surfaces is extremely difficult

## Recommended Documentation
Before appraisal, photograph each spirit cavity with:
- Direct flash to illuminate interior
- Ruler or scale object for size reference
- Multiple angles if possible
- Note any surviving traces of sealant, pigment, or contents
