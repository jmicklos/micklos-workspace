## The DIY Question — Can Jonathan do this himself?
**Verdict: Yes, technically — but the brick piers are the real skill gap and the hardest part of this specific build.**

### What his background covers well
- Excavation & layout (string lines, squaring, spacing)
- Footings / base prep (compaction, load paths, why the base matters)
- Reading grade, moving heavy material
- (If his retaining walls were dry-stacked segmental block, he never touched mortar — which is exactly the named gap.)

### What's genuinely new: the brick piers
- Freestanding brick piers are one of the *harder* masonry tasks — harder than a wall.
- A wall references itself; **a pier must be plumb on all four faces** over its full height with no reference. Errors compound fast.
- **Mortar consistency** (joint thickness, striking, workability) is a feel built over reps. First piers look like first piers.
- Iron panels **embed/anchor into the piers** — pier spacing and plumb must be dead-on or panels won't seat level.

### Realistic paths
1. **Full DIY** — doable if willing to build a throwaway practice pier first and accept a learning curve on a highly visible front-of-house result.
2. **Hybrid (recommended)** — DIY excavation, footings, layout (his strengths); hire a mason for the piers; DIY the iron panel install. Best quality-for-effort; the piers are the one thing you can't easily redo.
3. **Cheat the mortar** — CMU block core, core-filled with rebar + concrete, then brick veneer. More forgiving than solid structural brick.

### CMU explained (for reference)
CMU = Concrete Masonry Unit = essentially cinderblock (modern ones are concrete aggregate, not coal cinders). For a pier: stack hollow CMU, drop rebar down the cores, grout-fill them, then brick-veneer the outside. Block does the structural work and keeps you plumb; brick is the pretty face. More forgiving than laying solid structural brick.

### Findings — Code constraints (the design drivers)
- **FRONT setback fence max = 4 ft.** This is the binding constraint on the whole design. (SMC 23.44.014; SDCI Fences page.)
- **Brick piers get NO height exemption.** Unlike some cities, Seattle holds masonry piers to the same 4 ft cap in the front setback. So piers ≤4 ft too.
- **No permit required** as long as: total height ≤4 ft in front AND no masonry element over 6 ft. (Masonry/concrete over 6 ft anywhere triggers a construction permit even when wood wouldn't.)
- **Sight triangles** (SMC 23.54.030): fence can't obstruct vision clearance at the driveway or street corner — may need to drop height further there.
- **Right-of-way:** the 4 ft fence can sit in the front setback up to the property line, but NOT in the public ROW. The parking strip/sidewalk zone is likely city ROW — confirm exact property line vs. ROW before siting piers.
- For contrast: side/rear yards allow 6 ft solid, up to 8 ft total. Only the *front* is capped at 4 ft.
- **Verify directly before building:** exact NR sub-zone (NR1/2/3), any ECA/flood overlay, and the precise ROW line. SDCI: (206) 684-8600.

#### Finials, caps & light fixtures — do they count toward the 4 ft?
- **Decorative pier caps / finials: YES, counted.** Height is measured to the highest point **"including post caps"** (SMC 23.44.014). The only ornamentation exception (horizontal features ≤10") is tied to the 8 ft side/rear context, NOT the 4 ft front setback — so no headroom above 4 ft for finials.
  - **Design rule:** build pier body ~42–44" so the cap/finial brings TOTAL to ≤4 ft. Don't build piers to 4 ft then add a cap on top.
- **Light fixtures on piers: AMBIGUOUS — not addressed in code.** No "lamp post = exempt structure" rule, but also nothing explicitly counting a light as fence. It's an SDCI interpretation call. If pier lights matter, get a **written interpretation from SDCI** before building (206-684-8600 or send-a-question form at seattle.gov/sdci) — a verbal OK won't protect against a later complaint.
  - Consult SDCI Tip #220 (Lot Coverage, Height, Yard & Garage Standards); grade/measurement rules in SMC 23.86.

#### Going taller than 4 ft — the legal paths (researched 2026-07-16)
The 4 ft cap only applies *inside* the required front setback. Options to get a "proper" ~6 ft fence:
- **Set it back 15 ft (BY RIGHT — the realistic path).** NR front setback is **15 ft** (SMC 23.44.014 Table A; averaging does NOT apply here — only exception is Queen Anne Blvd landmark ROW). Behind the 15 ft line, a **6 ft fence is legal by right** (+ up to 2 ft architectural features), just a minor construction permit. Tradeoff: it's an interior garden wall 15 ft into the yard, NOT a street-edge statement.
- **Variance (technically yes, realistically no).** Type II Master Use Permit (public notice, 14-day neighbor appeal, months, hourly fees). Legal bar = **undue hardship from a unique physical site condition** (odd lot/topography), NOT owner preference. Aesthetics-driven requests are routinely **denied**. Don't pursue unless a genuine topographic hardship exists.
- **Grade + retaining wall (fits Jonathan's skillset).** Height measured from grade. A **grade-raising retaining wall + fence on top is allowed up to a combined 9'6"**. If the front slopes (sidewalk-repair notes suggest grade issues near the Hornbeam), a modest wall could legally buy real visual height — and retaining walls are his wheelhouse. Still lives behind the 15 ft line for the taller portion. (SMC 23.86 measurement.)
- **Living hedge (unregulated).** A hedge is NOT a fence — dense evergreen at the property line gives 6 ft+ screening at the sidewalk with no height limit. Pairs well with a 4 ft iron-and-brick fence in front.

**Decision needed from Jonathan:** what's the actual goal — *street-edge presence* (→ 4 ft at sidewalk), *privacy/screening* (→ hedge, or set-back wall), or *max height* (→ 6 ft set 15 ft back, possibly with grade/retaining wall)? These point to different designs.
**Verify:** exact zone NR1/2/3, dwelling-unit count, and whether the front actually slopes.

### Findings — Placement & pier constraints (researched 2026-07-16)
**Fences have NO setback in Seattle** — placement is generous; the real constraint is footings vs. the neighbor's line.
- **Where it can go:** anywhere on the lot, including directly ON the side and rear property lines (fences are exempt from yard setbacks; SDCI Property Line Disputes; SMC 23.44.014). Front: at the front property line behind the sidewalk, NOT in the ROW. Can run line-to-line across the front and down the sides.
- **Piers = part of the fence:** same on-the-line freedom, same height caps (4 ft front / 6 ft side-rear). No side-yard setback applies to them. NR structure side setback is 5 ft (SMC 23.44.010) but does NOT apply to fences/piers.
- **No code limit on pier size, shape, footprint, or spacing.** Below 6 ft there's no solid-vs-open ratio — piers can be as chunky as desired. (Open ~50% rule only kicks in above 6 ft.)
- **Permit trigger:** only if fence >8 ft OR any masonry element >6 ft. 4 ft front piers are nowhere near it.

**Neighbor-line issue — THE one that bites (action required):**
1. **Footing must stay entirely on his side of the line.** A pier's concrete footing is wider than the pier; if the pier face is on the line, the footing encroaches under the neighbor's soil = trespass (city won't stop it — private civil matter — but neighbor can). Fix: **inset the pier** so the footing stays on-parcel, OR pour an **eccentric/one-sided footing**, OR get written neighbor agreement.
2. **Don't center a pier on the shared line without written consent.** Straddling pier = boundary fence; masonry on their land needs agreement. WA good-neighbor law (RCW 16.60) does NOT force a residential neighbor to consent/pay. **Practical rule: build wholly on his side, footing included.**
3. Survey already shows a fence **"ON-LINE"** at the boundary → possible existing understanding with neighbor. Have the conversation before pouring.

**Front corner pier:** where front fence meets side line near the driveway, watch the **sight triangle** (SMC 23.54.030) — solid masonry in the 32"–82" band could obstruct driveway visibility. Residential exception exists for <3 parking spaces; confirm with SDCI.

**Flags for an SDCI call:** (a) very large pier could theoretically be reclassified as a freestanding wall (still no side setback, same height caps); (b) corner-pier sight-triangle at the driveway. Exact ROW + side-line positions need the survey; on-line piers need a neighbor agreement.
