# Wedding – Groom & Groomsmen Attire

> **Event:** December 12, 2026 — Casino Español, Salón Los Reyes, CDMX
> **Dress code (guests):** Black tie optional
> **Wedding party:** Full tuxedo, no exceptions
> **Ceremony:** 5:00 PM, golden-hour daylight, main floor
> **Reception:** 6:30 PM–midnight, indoors, one floor up

## Definition of Done
Groom's two looks and all 6 groomsmen's tuxedos are sourced, fitted, altered, and physically in hand (in CDMX or in a garment bag ready to fly). No outstanding measurements, orders, or alterations.

## The Groom — two looks

**Ceremony (5:00 PM):** Black tuxedo, shawl lapel, white bow tie.
**Reception (post-dinner):** White/ivory dinner jacket, shawl lapel, black formal trousers.

### Decided
- **Shawl lapel on both jackets** — consistent silhouette across the change, so the two looks read as one system.
- **Ceremony: black jacket + white bow tie. Reception: ivory jacket + BLACK bow tie.** ✓ Confirmed 2026-08-14. The inversion (white-on-black → black-on-white) makes the two looks read as one deliberate system, avoids the zero-contrast white-on-white torso that flattens under reception lighting, and lands the second look as canonical warm-weather black tie.
- **Groom changes; bride changes.** Both dress changes happen in the same window. Coordinate with [[Wedding – CDMX Run-of-Show]] — bride's change is ~15–20 min, timing TBD (between main and dessert, or after dinner before dancing).

### Note on the white bow tie (ceremony)
A white bow tie is traditionally white-tie territory (tailcoat + white piqué waistcoat), not black tie. With a black dinner jacket it is a deliberate break, not a classical pairing. It is **not a problem here** — it reads red-carpet/Hollywood, and at a black-tie-*optional* destination wedding that's a fine register. It also does real functional work: it makes the groom instantly identifiable in every ceremony frame against 6 black-tied groomsmen.

To make it read intentional rather than accidental, support it:
- **Marcella / piqué bib-front shirt** (not pleated). The piqué bib is the same fabric family as a white-tie shirt front, so the white tie has a partner on the shirt instead of floating alone.
- **Self-tie, not pre-tied.** A pre-tied white bow tie is the single fastest way to make the look read costume.
- **Keep the waist covered** — black cummerbund. The white tie already pulls the eye up; an exposed shirt-and-waistband kills it.

## The Groomsmen — recommendation

**Peak lapels do not clash.** Shawl and peak are both canonical black-tie lapels. The only genuinely incorrect lapel on a dinner jacket is a **notch** — that's a business-suit detail and it's the default on cheap rentals, so it has to be explicitly excluded.

More usefully: **lapel shape is the least visible differentiator in photographs.** At any real viewing distance, nobody registers shawl-vs-peak. The bow tie color, the boutonniere, and (later) the white jacket do all the actual work of separating the groom from the line. So the lapel decision should be made on **sourcing consistency**, not aesthetics.

### Recommended standard — all 6 groomsmen
| Element | Spec |
|---|---|
| Jacket | Black tuxedo, **peak lapel**, silk-faced (satin or grosgrain — pick one, all six match) |
| Lapel | **Peak preferred. Notch is disqualifying.** Shawl also fine if the vendor's shawl SKU is better stocked |
| Trousers | Matching black, single satin/grosgrain braid on the outseam, no cuffs, no belt loops |
| Shirt | White, **turndown collar**, pleated or marcella bib, French cuffs |
| Bow tie | **Black**, self-tie, matching the lapel facing material |
| Waist | Black cummerbund (pleats facing up) or low-cut waistcoat — **not optional**, this is the most-skipped element |
| Shoes | Black patent leather or highly polished black calf oxfords |
| Socks | Black, over-the-calf |

**Peak over shawl for the groomsmen, because:**
1. Peak is the most consistently stocked tuxedo lapel across rental and off-the-rack vendors — six guys in six cities can actually match on it.
2. It's unambiguously black-tie-correct, so there's no risk of a traditionalist read.
3. The subtle contrast against Jonathan's shawl reads as intentional *because* the white bow tie is already carrying the differentiation. Lapel shape isn't asked to do work it can't do.

**Black bow ties on all groomsmen is the non-negotiable part.** If groomsmen also wore white bow ties, the groom's ceremony look would be indistinguishable from theirs.

## Risks & Constraints

### 1. Shade drift — the real failure mode
Six "black" tuxedos from six different sources will not be the same black. Real-world spread runs jet → charcoal → sun-faded → midnight blue. Under flash and reception lighting this is glaring and unfixable in post. **Mitigation: one vendor, one SKU, one consolidated order.** Do not let anyone "already have a tux."

### 2. Destination logistics — buy probably beats rent
Rental houses (The Black Tux, Generation Tux, Menguin) ship to US addresses and require timely US returns. Taking rental garments to Mexico and returning them from abroad is a real problem — late/lost-return fees land on whoever ordered.
- **Option A — rent to US home, fly with it, return after.** Cheapest (~$180–250/person) but 6 return deadlines to chase post-honeymoon, and no margin if a bag is lost.
- **Option B — everyone buys** (SuitSupply, ~$600–800; less on sale). ~$400 delta per person, but zero return logistics, better fit, and they keep it. **Recommended for a destination wedding.**
- Either way: **groomsmen fly with tuxedos in a garment bag as carry-on.** Nothing critical goes in checked luggage.

### 3. Timeline — 4 months out, this is tighter than it looks
- Made-to-measure / online custom: **10–16 weeks**. Must order by **early September** to be safe.
- Off-the-rack + alterations: order by mid-October, alterations 2–3 weeks.
- Rental: measurements collected by early October, garments arrive ~2 weeks out.
- **Getting 6 men in 6 cities to submit measurements is the long pole**, not the garments.

### 5. Two things the palette actually creates
**Pocket squares → white linen, flat/TV fold. Not taupe.**
Taupe on black is a low-contrast, muddy combination — at photographic distance it reads as a smudge on the chest rather than an accent. White linen on black is high contrast and crisp, and it's the black-tie canonical choice anyway. Echoing the bridesmaid color on the men is a wedding-industry reflex that reads dated; the pairing already works by contrast. **Confirm with Wan Ting that she isn't expecting taupe ties/squares on the men.**

**Groom's reception boutonniere → drop it, or go greenery-only.**
White florals on a **black** lapel is high contrast and looks excellent — that's the groomsmen, and the groom at the ceremony. But a white boutonniere on an **ivory** jacket disappears entirely. Two options: drop the boutonniere at the jacket change (defensible — the boutonniere is a ceremony item and by the reception it's usually crushed anyway), or have the florist make a **greenery-forward** second boutonniere that reads against ivory. Recommend just dropping it.

## Tradition & etiquette — who pays for what

### The traditional split
**Groomsmen pay for their own formalwear** (rental or purchase). That's long-standing American wedding etiquette and it still holds.

**The couple pays for all wedding party flowers** — every boutonniere and bouquet. This one is firm, not optional.

### Bow ties: yes, buy them
Two independent reasons, and the second is the stronger one:

1. **It's the modern norm.** The convention that's hardened over the last couple decades is that the couple buys the accessories that *must match exactly* — ties, pocket squares, sometimes studs/cufflinks — because exact uniformity is the couple's aesthetic requirement, not the groomsmen's.
2. **It's the only way to actually get six identical bow ties.** Left to six men ordering separately you will get a mix of satin and grosgrain, different blacks, and at least one pre-tied. Buying them centrally is the same shade-drift mitigation as the one-vendor-one-SKU rule for the jackets — just cheaper to enforce. ~$25–40 × 6 = **$150–240 total.** Cheap insurance.

**Buy the pocket squares too**, same logic, and they're ~$10 each.

**Weight this against the ask you're already making.** This is a destination wedding — six men are buying flights to CDMX, paying for lodging, taking time off, and (if you go the buy route) spending $600–800 on a tuxedo. That's a heavy lift. Covering accessories offsets it. Consider whether to subsidize further.

### Other traditions worth upholding here
- **Groomsmen gift — make it the studs and cufflinks.** Classic tradition, but here it's *functional*: a tuxedo shirt requires studs and cufflinks and most men don't own a set. The gift solves a real logistics problem and guarantees the detail matches. Given at the rehearsal dinner or the getting-ready morning.
- **A real gift for Lauren.** She's officiating *and* writing the ceremony herself — that's substantial unpaid creative labor, well beyond a normal officiant's role. Traditionally the couple gives the officiant a gift or honorarium; this case warrants more than the default.
- **Rehearsal dinner is traditionally hosted by the groom's parents** — Dan + Janet. Worth an explicit conversation rather than an assumption, especially at a destination wedding where the cost structure is different. Connects to the open "figure out day before and day after" task in [[Wedding – CDMX Wedding]].
- **Groom traditionally covers the marriage license and officiant fee.**
- **Something inherited in the outfit.** A common groom tradition is wearing a father's or grandfather's cufflinks or watch. Dan is alive, present, and giving a toast — this is an easy, genuine one.
- **Boutonniere on the left lapel**, in the buttonhole, over the heart. Requires a **working lapel buttonhole** — good dinner jackets have one, cheap ones don't. Add it to the spec.
- **Groom's boutonniere differs** from the groomsmen's — traditionally slightly larger or a distinct bloom.
