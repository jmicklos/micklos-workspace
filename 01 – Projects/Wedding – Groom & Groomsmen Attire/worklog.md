# Worklog — Wedding – Groom & Groomsmen Attire

## 2026-08-14 — Project created; lapel question answered

### Situation
Jonathan asked for a new project covering his own wedding attire and the groomsmen's. Wedding is Dec 12, 2026 at Casino Español, CDMX — about 4 months out at time of writing. Dress code for guests is **black tie optional**; the wedding party will be in full tuxedos.

Jonathan arrived with a formed plan for himself and one specific question about the groomsmen:
- His plan: **black tuxedo, shawl lapel, white bow tie** for the ceremony, then switch to a **white tuxedo, also shawl lapel**, for later in the evening.
- His question: "What should the groomsmen do? Would peak lapels clash?"

Verified no existing attire project before creating (searched active projects, backlog, and archive for attire/tux/suit/groom/dress/wardrobe/outfit/style). Nothing. The archived `Wedding – Invite Groomsmen` project covers only the asks, not clothing.

### Approach & Rationale
Answered the lapel question directly with mechanism rather than taste, per how Jonathan reads (evidence/mechanism-first, repelled by rhetoric). Core answer given:

**Peak lapels do not clash.** Shawl and peak are both canonical black-tie lapels. The only wrong lapel on a dinner jacket is a **notch** — a business-suit detail that is the silent default on cheap rentals, so it must be explicitly excluded from any spec sent out.

The more useful reframe offered: **lapel shape is the least visible differentiator in a photograph.** At real viewing distance nobody registers shawl-vs-peak. The bow tie color, the boutonniere, and the white jacket do all the actual work of separating groom from line. So the lapel decision should be made on **sourcing consistency**, not aesthetics.

Recommended **peak** for the groomsmen, on three grounds:
1. Peak is the most consistently stocked tuxedo lapel across vendors — six men in six cities can actually match on it.
2. Unambiguously black-tie-correct; no traditionalist risk.
3. The contrast against Jonathan's shawl reads intentional *because* the white bow tie already carries the differentiation — the lapel isn't asked to do work it can't do.

Flagged **black bow ties on all groomsmen as non-negotiable** — if they also wore white, Jonathan's ceremony look would be indistinguishable from theirs.

### Two things raised that Jonathan did not ask about
**1. The white bow tie is a break from black-tie canon.** A white bow tie is white-tie territory (tailcoat + white piqué waistcoat). With a black dinner jacket it's a deliberate break. Did NOT try to talk him out of it — it reads red-carpet/Hollywood, which is a fine register for a black-tie-*optional* destination wedding, and it does real functional work (instant identifiability against 6 black-tied groomsmen). Instead gave three supports to make it read intentional rather than costume: marcella/piqué bib-front shirt (gives the white tie a partner on the shirt), self-tie not pre-tied, and keep the waist covered with a black cummerbund.

**2. The second look needs a bow tie decision he hadn't stated.** Recommended **inverting**: white/ivory jacket + **black** bow tie. Reasoning: white jacket + white tie gives zero torso contrast and flattens the upper half in reception lighting; inverting makes the two looks legible as one deliberate system (white-on-black → black-on-white); and ivory jacket + black tie + black trousers is the canonical warm-weather black-tie variant, so it lands correct as well as intentional. **This is the main open decision.**

### Risks identified (Jonathan didn't ask, but these are the ones that actually bite)
1. **Shade drift.** Six "black" tuxedos from six sources will not be the same black — jet/charcoal/faded/midnight-blue spread is glaring under flash and unfixable in post. Mitigation: one vendor, one SKU, one consolidated order. Nobody uses a tux they already own.
2. **Destination rental logistics.** Rental houses ship to US addresses and need timely US returns; returning from Mexico post-wedding is a genuine problem and the fees land on whoever ordered. Recommended **buying** (SuitSupply ~$600–800) over renting (~$180–250) — ~$400/person delta buys away all return logistics and gets better fit. Either way: carry-on garment bag, never checked.
3. **Timeline is tighter than 4 months suggests.** MTM is 10–16 weeks → order by early September. The long pole is not the garments, it's **getting 6 men in 6 cities to submit measurements.**

| Element | Color |
|---|---|
| Bridesmaid dresses (5) | Tan / taupe |
| Florals | White + greenery flourishes |
| Table linens | Creamy white |

Answer given: **yes, that covers it for attire purposes, and nothing on the men's side needs to change.** Black tuxedos against tan/taupe is a standard high-functioning pairing — the men are the neutral dark frame, the bridesmaids carry the warmth. Explicitly advised **against** shifting anything on the men toward taupe to "match."

But it created two derived decisions that Jonathan hadn't raised:

**1. Pocket squares → white linen, NOT taupe.** Mechanism given: taupe on black is low-contrast and muddy; at photographic distance it reads as a smudge on the chest rather than an accent. White linen on black is high-contrast, crisp, and canonical for black tie anyway. Echoing the bridesmaid color on the men is a wedding-industry reflex that reads dated — the pairing already works by contrast. **Action flagged: confirm with Wan Ting she isn't expecting taupe on the men**, since this is her palette.

**2. The reception boutonniere breaks.** White florals on a *black* lapel is high contrast and excellent — that covers the groomsmen and Jonathan at the ceremony. But a white boutonniere on an *ivory* jacket disappears completely. Two options offered: drop it at the jacket change (recommended — it's a ceremony item and is usually crushed by the reception anyway), or commission a greenery-forward second boutonniere.

### Tradition answer — bow ties: yes, buy them
Gave the traditional baseline first: **groomsmen pay for their own formalwear** (still holds), but **the couple pays for all wedding party flowers** — every boutonniere and bouquet, firm.

On bow ties specifically, two independent arguments, with the second framed as stronger:
1. **Modern norm** — the couple buys the accessories that must match *exactly* (ties, pocket squares, sometimes studs/cufflinks), because exact uniformity is the couple's aesthetic requirement, not the groomsmen's.
2. **It's the only way to actually get six identical bow ties.** Six men ordering separately produces a mix of satin and grosgrain, different blacks, and at least one pre-tied. This is the same shade-drift failure mode already flagged for the jackets, just far cheaper to enforce — ~$25–40 × 6 = $150–240.

Also recommended buying the pocket squares (~$10 each, same logic).

**Framing point made deliberately:** weighed this against the ask already on the table. Six men are buying flights to CDMX, lodging, time off, plus $600–800 for a tuxedo if the buy route is chosen. That's heavy. Covering accessories offsets it, and suggested Jonathan consider subsidizing further. Left as an open decision rather than a recommendation.

### Other traditions surfaced — selected, not listicled
Filtered for what's actually actionable for *this* wedding rather than listing generic groom traditions:
- **Groomsmen gift = studs + cufflinks.** Classic, but pitched on function: a tuxedo shirt *requires* studs and cufflinks, most men don't own a set, so the gift simultaneously solves a logistics problem and guarantees the detail matches.
- **A real gift for Lauren.** She's officiating *and* writing the ceremony herself — substantial unpaid creative labor beyond a normal officiant role. Warrants more than the default officiant gift.
- **Rehearsal dinner is traditionally hosted by the groom's parents** (Dan + Janet). Flagged as needing an explicit conversation, not an assumption, because destination-wedding cost structure is different. Ties to the existing open "figure out day before and day after" task in the parent project.
- **Marriage license + officiant fee** traditionally the groom's.
- **Something inherited in the outfit** — father's/grandfather's cufflinks or watch. Called out as easy and genuine here because Dan is alive, present, and giving a toast.
- **Working lapel buttonhole** required to actually wear a boutonniere; cheap tuxedos lack one. Added to the groomsmen spec.
- **Groom's boutonniere differs** from the groomsmen's (larger or distinct bloom).

Noted the traditions already consciously skipped per the Run-of-Show — bouquet toss, garter toss, staged exit, not-seeing-the-bride (overridden by the first look). All deliberate, no action.
